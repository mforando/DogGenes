What’s in a name?

276,903 New York City dogs and their names

Every dog in New York City needs a license, and the city publishes them. We cleaned up the records and asked two questions: can you guess a dog’s breed from its name? And which names are most characteristic of tiny dogs, giant dogs, sled dogs, or herders?

Most popular names: Bella (2,976), Luna (2,711), Max (2,568), Charlie (2,294), Coco (2,266), Lola (1,822), Milo (1,808), Rocky (1,767), Teddy (1,748), Lucy (1,525)

Can a name predict the breed? Mostly no.

We trained a model to guess a dog’s breed using only its name, from the spelling down to fragments like “-ki” or “Chi-”, and tested it on 37,124 dogs it had never seen. It picks the right breed out of the 20 most common 16% of the time, compared with 11% if you always guessed the most common breed. That’s about 1.5× better than guessing, but still wrong more than four times in five. For size and original job, the name tells you almost nothing.

Why so weak? The most popular names, like Bella, Luna, Max and Charlie, are used for every kind of dog, and they cover most of the city. The signal lives in rarer names: a dog called Kitsune or Hachi is very likely a Shiba Inu, but only a few hundred dogs carry names like that.

What the names say about ancestry

  • Owners echo a breed’s homeland. Shiba Inus get Japanese names (Kuma, Hiro, Yuki, Kenji, Haru), Chihuahuas get Spanish ones (Chico, Chiquita, Papi), French bulldogs get French ones (Hugo, Louis, Napoleon), and schnauzers and dachshunds get German ones (Fritz, Otto, Heidi).
  • Size shows up as attitude. Giant breeds get big, sturdy names (Bernie, Hank, Moose, Tank, Diesel). Toy breeds get royal and dainty ones (Princess, Prince, Chanel, Minnie, Tiny).
  • Looks and jobs leave a trace. White dogs are Snowball, Casper and Snowy; huskies and other sled dogs are Balto, Ghost, Lobo and Ice; dachshunds are Frankie, Oscar, Pickle and, almost certainly in honor of the Coney Island hot dog stand, Nathan.

Data: NYC Dog Licensing Dataset (NYC Department of Health, NYC Open Data), 819,323 license records from 2016–2026. A renewal creates a new record, so dogs were de-duplicated by name, sex, birth year, breed and ZIP code (357,676 dogs). We dropped blank or placeholder names and names that were just a breed (“Yorkie”, “Husky”), and kept dogs whose breed we could match, 276,903 in all. Mixes count under their main breed. Breeds are as reported by owners; sizes are typical adult weights for the breed. Distinctive names use a log-odds ratio with an informative Dirichlet prior (Monroe et al. 2008). The model is a logistic regression on letter patterns, scored on a held-out 20% of dogs.
