The dog breed family tree · a beginner’s guide to dog DNA

How to read the dog’s family tree

In 2017, scientists analyzed the DNA of 1,355 dogs from 161 breeds and drew a single family tree showing how the breeds are related. It is elegant, and easy to misread. Here is how to read it, one step at a time. No science background needed.

Parker, Dreger, Rimbault, Davis, Mullen, Carpintero-Ramirez & Ostrander, “Genomic Analyses Reveal the Influence of Geographic Origin, Migration, and Hybridization on Modern Dog Breed Development,” Cell Reports 19 (2017).

Quick glossary

Five words you’ll need

A few terms come up often. Hover over or tap any underlined word to see its definition again.

  • DNA

    The instruction manual inside every cell, written in a four-letter alphabet: A, C, G and T. A dog’s copy is about 2.4 billion letters long.

    Any two dogs match on almost all of it. The researchers compared about 150,000 of the spots where dogs tend to differ.

  • CladogramCLAD-oh-gram

    A family-tree diagram. It shows who split off from whom, and in what order. Think of a sports bracket run in reverse.

    It doesn’t show dates, and the length of a line doesn’t mean anything.

  • Claderhymes with “made”

    One branch of the tree plus everything growing out of it: a shared ancestor and all of its descendants.

    Like one side of your family: you, your siblings, your cousins, and the grandparents you have in common.

  • Bootstrapping

    A way of checking whether you’d get the same answer twice. The researchers reshuffled their data and rebuilt the tree 100 times.

    If two breeds end up paired in 95 of those 100 trees, the pairing gets a 95% score. Higher means more trustworthy.

  • HaplotypeHAP-lo-type

    A chunk of DNA passed down in one piece, like a paragraph copied word-for-word from a parent. Every generation, the chunks get cut into smaller pieces as each parent’s DNA is shuffled.

    So when two dogs share a long chunk, they had a common ancestor not long ago. Lengths are given in Mb, millions of DNA letters.

The big picture

Read it from the center out

Each sliver on the rim is a group of dogs whose DNA was tested, 1,355 in all. Lines connect them by genetic similarity: the more alike two dogs’ DNA, the closer they sit. The chart is a cladogram, a family tree that shows the order in which groups split apart, but not when. Line lengths carry no meaning.

The center is the oldest split, anchored by the golden jackal, a wild relative. Next come gray wolves, dogs’ closest living relatives, then the basenji, an ancient African breed. The farther a branch sits from the center, the more recent the split.

The edge

Each wedge is a breed

The 10 boxers in the study have DNA so similar that they always cluster, so they are drawn as one wedge; each dot is a dog. Nearly every breed behaves the same way: 146 of 161 form their own tidy wedge. Breeds, in other words, are genetic islands.

Who’s related to whom

Follow the lines to where they meet

To see how closely two breeds are related, trace each one inward until their lines meet. That junction is their most recent shared ancestor; the sooner the lines meet, the closer the relationship.

The golden retriever’s nearest relative here is the flat-coated retriever, followed by the Labrador and, further back, the Newfoundland.

Watch out

Neighbors aren’t necessarily family

The dachshund and the Afghan hound sit side by side on the rim, and both are called hounds. But their lines don’t meet until deep in the tree.

Think of the tree as a hanging mobile: any branch can spin on its hook without changing who is related to whom. Spin this one and the two breeds land on opposite sides of the circle, with exactly the same path to their common ancestor. The order around the rim is a drawing choice. Only the branching counts.

How sure are they?

Little markers rate the confidence

The researchers rebuilt the tree 100 times from resampled data, a test called bootstrapping. Each marker shows how often a fork came out the same way:

  • 90–100 times out of 100: rock solid
  • 70–89 times: fairly sure
  • 50–69 times: a reasonable guess

Unmarked forks appeared less than half the time. Most sit near the center, so the oldest splits are the least certain.

Family groups

Breeds fall into 23 families

Pull back, and breeds group into larger branches called clades: a shared ancestor and all of its descendants. Almost every breed, 150 in all, falls into one of 23 clades of two to 18 breeds. The gray wedges are loners that fit no group.

Most groupings are intuitive: terriers with terriers, retrievers with retrievers. Some are not. The Mediterranean clade pairs sighthounds like the saluki with livestock guardians like the Great Pyrenees; the UK Rural clade pairs greyhounds with collies. The likely explanation is that each region bred its hunting dogs and its farm dogs from the same local stock. Origin mattered more than job.

A surprise

Why so many breeds are European

About 70 percent of the populations on the globe (115 of 164) are European. That reflects history more than biology. The idea of a breed, with a written standard and a closed studbook, began in Victorian Britain and spread through kennel clubs in Europe and North America, and the study drew mostly on breeds those clubs recognize. Most of the world’s dogs are village dogs that belong to no breed.

Shared DNA follows the map, too: about three-quarters of it links breeds from the same continent. Asian breeds keep largely to themselves. Breeds from the Americas, Australia and Africa trace back mostly to European dogs brought by settlers; the Australian kelpie’s closest relative is the border collie. Rule breakers

Breeds that won’t sit together

A few breeds are scattered across more than one wedge. Some are new breeds still taking shape. Others split by origin: cane corsos from Italy cluster together, while American ones sit beside Neapolitan mastiffs.

The Xoloitzcuintli (Mexican hairless dog) and the Peruvian hairless dog are spread across distant branches, some beside the German shepherd, a sign of past mixing with European herding dogs.

Gaps in the record

The missing link

Every fork in the tree is an ancestor no one has seen. The researchers had DNA only from living dogs and inferred each fork from its descendants. In human evolution, such ancestors are called missing links.

Dogs have one, too: the extinct Ice Age wolf population they came from. If its DNA were found, it would join the tree at the fork where dogs part from wolves. Watch the chart: the breeds shift to make room, but none changes place.

The DNA web · The ring

The tree, bent into a ring

Part two. A family tree shows how breeds branched apart. But breeders also crossed breeds back together, leaving long shared stretches of DNA. The study drew those connections as a chord diagram: a ring of breeds tied together by ribbons.

The ring has 168 blocks, one per breed, in the same order and colors as the tree, starting from the wolf just left of the top and running counterclockwise. Every block is the same size, however many dogs were tested. Three breeds appear twice because they were sampled in two countries: the Tibetan mastiff, the saluki and the cane corso.

The DNA web · The ribbons

A ribbon marks a recent cross

Each generation, the DNA a puppy inherits is cut into smaller pieces, called haplotypes. When dogs from two breeds share long pieces, they had a common ancestor recently, usually because someone crossed the breeds.

Breeds in the same family share plenty of DNA anyway, so the chart shows only pairs from different families, and only the top 5 percent of those: 190 ribbons. The Eurasier, created in the 1970s from the chow chow, keeshond and Samoyed, has three, one to each parent.

The DNA web · Ribbon width

Thicker ribbon, more in common

Wider ribbons mean more shared DNA. The Eurasier and chow chow share about 179 million DNA letters (179 Mb), the most of any pair; the Berger Picard and Bouvier des Flandres share 78 Mb. The thin ribbon between the Italian cane corso and the Saint Bernard, under 10 Mb, barely qualified.

Compare ribbons with one another, not with block size: a breed with many ribbons must fit them all into one block.

The DNA web · Super-connectors

A handful of breeds hold the web together

About half the breeds have one ribbon or none. Six connect to more than eight breeds outside their family: the toy Manchester terrier, pug, Chinook, Airedale, German shepherd and Berger Picard. Each ribbon takes the color of its busier end, so a fan of one color points back to a hub.

A hub is usually either a recent blend, like the Chinook, bred from sled dogs and shepherds in the 1910s, or a favorite ingredient. The German shepherd, created in the late 1800s, is the second kind: its ribbons reach the Belgian Malinois, Doberman, Leonberger and Italian cane corso, among others.

The DNA web · A well-traveled breed

The pug gets around

On the tree, the pug sits beside the Brussels griffon. Its ribbons tell a broader story, reaching Asian lapdogs like the Pekingese, shih tzu and Lhasa apso, and small breeds around the ring. The researchers think pugs left Asia early and were then used to build many small European breeds.

The DNA web · Same breed, different story

A tale of two cane corsos

The two cane corso blocks diverge. Dogs sampled in Italy (ITCC) connect to the German shepherd and Chinook; dogs sampled in the United States (CANE) connect to the Rottweiler, a link the Italian dogs lack. The breed has been in the United States for only about 30 years, and American dogs have already mixed with local breeds.

The DNA web · The quiet ones

No ribbons tells a story too

The 55 highlighted blocks have no ribbons at all. They include most Mediterranean breeds (salukis, Afghan hounds, Great Pyrenees), most scent hounds, like the basset and beagle, and all three poodles.

No ribbons means no recent mixing with other families. The researchers think the Mediterranean and Asian spitz groups, which share the least, may be among the oldest dog families.

The hidden web

The web, routed through the tree

Here is the same data, drawn another way. In edge bundling, each connection leaves one breed, follows the tree’s branches in toward the two breeds’ shared ancestor, then travels out to the other. Connections that share branches merge into thick bundles; a lone thin curve is a one-off cross.

The picture combines the ancient family tree with two centuries of crossbreeding: 117 breeds show recent mixing with other families. It also shows why a tree can mislead. The Eurasier lands in a wedge of its own between its three parent breeds, because a tree can only split, not merge.
