A beginner’s guide to dog DNA

How to read the dog’s family tree

In 2017, scientists tested the DNA of 1,355 dogs from 161 breeds and drew one giant family tree showing how they’re all related. It’s gorgeous, and very easy to misread. Scroll down and we’ll walk through it one step at a time. No science background needed.

Parker, Dreger, Rimbault, Davis, Mullen, Carpintero-Ramirez & Ostrander, “Genomic Analyses Reveal the Influence of Geographic Origin, Migration, and Hybridization on Modern Dog Breed Development,” Cell Reports 19 (2017).

Quick glossary

Five words you’ll need

The chart uses a few science words. Here’s what they mean in plain English. You can come back here anytime.

  • DNA

    The instruction manual inside every cell, written in a four-letter alphabet: A, C, G and T. A dog’s copy is about 2.4 billion letters long.

    Any two dogs match on almost all of it. The researchers compared about 150,000 of the spots where dogs tend to differ.

  • CladogramCLAD-oh-gram

    A family-tree diagram. It shows who split off from whom, and in what order. Think of a sports bracket run in reverse.

    It doesn’t show dates, and the length of a line doesn’t mean anything.

  • Claderhymes with “made”

    One branch of the tree plus everything growing out of it: a shared ancestor and all of its descendants.

    Like one side of your family: you, your siblings, your cousins, and the grandparents you have in common.

  • Bootstrapping

    A way of checking whether you’d get the same answer twice. The researchers reshuffled their data and rebuilt the tree 100 times.

    If two breeds end up paired in 95 of those 100 trees, the pairing gets a 95% score. Higher means more trustworthy.

  • HaplotypeHAP-lo-type

    A chunk of DNA passed down in one piece, like a paragraph copied word-for-word from a parent. Every generation, the chunks get cut into smaller pieces as each parent’s DNA is shuffled.

    So when two dogs share a long chunk, they had a common ancestor not long ago. Lengths are given in Mb, millions of DNA letters.

The big picture

1,355 dogs in one circle

Every sliver around the edge is a group of real dogs whose DNA was tested. The lines inside connect dogs by how similar their DNA is. The more two dogs have in common, the closer together they sit.

It’s a cladogram: a family tree that shows who split off from whom. It doesn’t show dates, so don’t read anything into how long the lines are.

Where to start

Start in the middle

The very center is the root, the oldest split in the whole tree. To anchor it, the researchers included the golden jackal, a wild cousin of dogs. Because we know it branched off long ago, it tells us which end of the tree is the old end.

Next come grey wolves, dogs’ closest living relatives (cousins, not ancestors: dogs come from an Ice Age wolf population that has since died out), then the basenji, an ancient African breed. Every other breed is further out. The rule of thumb: the closer to the edge, the more recent the split.

The edge

Each wedge is a breed

Meet the boxers. All ten boxers in the study have such similar DNA that they always clump together, so they’re drawn as one wedge. Each little dot is one dog. Wider wedges mean more dogs were tested.

That’s true for almost every breed: 146 of 161 form their own tidy wedge. Breeds really are distinct genetic groups, like little islands.

Who’s related to whom

Follow the lines to where they meet

Want to know how closely two breeds are related? Trace each one inward until their lines join. That join is their shared ancestor. The sooner the lines meet, the closer the relatives.

The golden retriever’s closest relative here is the flat-coated retriever. Next comes the Labrador, and a bit further back the Newfoundland.

Watch out

Next-door neighbors can be strangers

The dachshund and the Afghan hound sit side by side on the edge, and both are called “hounds.” Easy to assume they’re close family, right?

Nope. Trace them inward and their lines don’t meet until deep in the tree. Sitting next to each other on the edge means nothing. Only where the lines join counts.

Why that happens

Every fork can spin

Think of the tree as a hanging mobile. You can spin any branch around its hook and nothing about who’s related to whom changes. Watch: we just spun the fork joining the dachshund and Afghan hound.

Now they’re on opposite sides of the circle, but their path to the shared ancestor is exactly the same. The order around the edge is a drawing choice. The branching is the real information.

How sure are they?

Little markers rate the confidence

The researchers double-checked their tree by bootstrapping: they reshuffled the data and rebuilt the tree 100 times. Each marker shows how often a fork came out the same way:

  • 90–100 times out of 100: rock solid
  • 70–89 times: pretty sure
  • 50–69 times: a decent hunch

No marker means it showed up less than half the time. Most of those are deep near the center, so take the oldest splits with a grain of salt.

Family groups

23 big family groups

Zoom out and the breeds clump into bigger branches called clades: a shared ancestor plus all its descendants. Almost every breed (150 of them) falls into one of 23 clades, each holding 2 to 18 breeds.

Most make intuitive sense: terriers with terriers, retrievers with retrievers. The gray wedges are loners that didn’t reliably fit into any group.

A surprise

Where they’re from beats what they do

Some families look like odd couples. The Mediterranean group puts speedy, skinny sighthounds like the saluki next to giant sheep guards like the Great Pyrenees. The UK Rural group pairs greyhounds with collies.

The likely story: each region bred its own hunting dogs and its own farm dogs from the same local stock. Home turf mattered more than the job.

Why does Europe dominate? About 70% of the breeds on the globe below (115 of 164) are European, and that says more about history than about dogs. The idea of a breed, with a written standard and a closed studbook, was invented in Victorian Britain: the first dog show was held in 1859 and the Kennel Club was founded in 1873, and kennel clubs across Europe and North America followed. Owners there turned their local working dogs into named breeds, so Europe’s regional dogs were recorded and kept apart. Elsewhere, most dogs were, and still are, free-roaming village dogs that belong to no breed at all. The study also drew mostly on breeds recognized by European and North American kennel clubs, so it reflects which dogs were registered, not where dogs live.

Shared DNA follows the map too. About three-quarters of all the DNA that breeds share in the study links breeds from the same continent. Partly that’s because most breeds are European, but Asia’s keep to themselves as well: more than half of their sharing is with other Asian breeds, like the Shih Tzu, Lhasa Apso and Pekingese. Breeds from the rest of the world mostly point back to Europe, because many were built from dogs that settlers brought with them. Only about a fifth of North America’s sharing stays on the continent, and less for Australia and Africa: the Australian kelpie’s closest relative is the border collie, and South Africa’s boerboel’s is the bullmastiff. Rule breakers

Breeds that won’t sit together

A few breeds are scattered across more than one wedge. Some are newer breeds still settling down. Others depend on where the dogs came from: cane corsos from Italy stick together, while American ones sit beside Neapolitan mastiffs.

The Xoloitzcuintli (Mexican hairless dog) and the Peruvian hairless dog are split across distant branches. Some of them sit right next to the German shepherd, a hint of old mixing with European herding dogs.

The DNA web · The ring

Every block is a breed

Part two. The family tree shows how breeds branched apart. But breeders also mix breeds back together, and that leaves long shared stretches of DNA behind. The paper drew those as a chord diagram: a ring of breeds tied together by ribbons. Here’s how to read it.

The circle is cut into 168 blocks, one per breed, each labelled with its name. The breeds sit in the same places as on the family tree, so the grey wolf and basenji are still at the top. Hover over any block for more about it.

Every breed gets the same size slice, no matter how many dogs were tested. A few breeds show up twice because dogs were collected in two countries: the Tibetan mastiff, saluki, and cane corso.

The DNA web · The order

Same order as the family tree

Start at the wolf, just left of the top, and go counter-clockwise. The breeds come in the same order as the edge of the family tree, with the same colors for each family group (clade).

So the ring is the family tree, flattened. Blocks next to each other are close relatives. Everything drawn inside the ring is new information the tree couldn’t show.

The DNA web · The ribbons

A ribbon means shared DNA

Every generation, the DNA a puppy inherits gets chopped into smaller chunks (haplotypes). So if dogs from two breeds share a big chunk, they had a common ancestor not long ago. That usually means someone crossed the breeds.

The Eurasier was made in the 1970s from the chow chow, keeshond, and samoyed. Sure enough, its three ribbons go straight to those three breeds.

The DNA web · Ribbon width

Thicker ribbon, more in common

Wider ribbons mean more shared DNA. The Eurasier and chow chow share about 179 million DNA letters (179 Mb), the most of any pair. The Berger Picard and Bouvier des Flandres share 78 Mb. The skinny ribbon between the Italian cane corso and Saint Bernard, at under 10 Mb, only just made the cut.

One catch: in the full picture, a breed with lots of ribbons has to squeeze them into one block. Compare ribbons to each other, not to the block size.

The DNA web · What gets a ribbon

Only the surprises

Breeds in the same family group share plenty of DNA anyway, so those pairs are skipped. A ribbon only appears when two breeds from different families share more than 95% of other such pairs. That leaves 190 ribbons.

What’s left is the interesting stuff: breeders crossing in a new trait, or dogs moving to a new country and mixing with the locals.

The DNA web · Ribbon color

Follow the color to the hub

Each ribbon takes the color of its busier end, the breed with more connections. A fan of same-colored ribbons spreading across the ring points back to one popular breed.

The German shepherd is a great example. Its ribbons reach the Belgian malinois, Doberman, Leonberger, Italian cane corso, and more. The breed was created in the late 1800s and has been a popular ingredient in other breeds ever since.

The DNA web · A well-traveled breed

The pug gets around

On the family tree, the pug sits with the Brussels griffon. But its ribbons tell a bigger story. They reach Asian lapdogs like the Pekingese, shih tzu, and Lhasa apso, plus small breeds all over the ring.

The researchers think pugs were shipped out of Asia early and then used to help create lots of small European breeds.

The DNA web · Super-connectors

Six breeds tie it all together

About half the breeds have one ribbon or none. Just six connect to more than eight breeds outside their family: the toy Manchester terrier, pug, chinook, Airedale, German shepherd, and Berger Picard.

That many ribbons usually means one of two things. The breed was recently built from lots of others, like the chinook, made from sled dogs and shepherds in the 1910s. Or it was itself a favorite ingredient for breeders.

The DNA web · Same breed, different story

A tale of two cane corsos

Compare the two cane corso blocks. Dogs from Italy (ITCC) connect to the German shepherd and chinook. Dogs from the United States (CANE) connect to the Rottweiler, and the Italian dogs don’t.

The breed has only been in the US for about 30 years, and the American dogs have already picked up some local mixing.

The DNA web · The quiet ones

No ribbons tells a story too

The 55 highlighted blocks have no ribbons at all. They include most of the Mediterranean breeds (salukis, Afghan hounds, Great Pyrenees), most scent hounds like the basset and beagle, and the standard, miniature, and toy poodles.

No ribbons means no recent mixing with other families. The researchers think the Mediterranean and Asian spitz groups, which share the least, may be among the oldest dog families of all.

Ribbons meet the tree

Routing the connections through the tree

The circle chart drew each connection as a ribbon straight across the middle. Here is another way to draw the very same data, called edge bundling: each curve leaves one breed, follows the family tree’s branches in toward the two breeds’ shared ancestor, then travels back out to the other breed.

Take the Eurasier again. A tree can only split, but this breed was made by joining three: in the 1970s breeders crossed the chow chow, keeshond and samoyed. Its three curves follow the tree to each parent, which is why it ends up in a wedge of its own somewhere between them.

The hidden web

Every crossing, bundled

Now every connection between breeds from different families at once. Curves that travel the same branches merge into thick bundles, like cables in a wall. A bundle shows a lot of mixing between two parts of the tree; a lone thin curve is a one-off cross.

Bundling trades the circle chart’s exact widths for structure: you can see the old family tree and the last 200 years of crossbreeding in a single picture. In all, 117 breeds show this kind of recent mixing.

Gaps in the record

The missing links

Every fork in this tree is an ancestor nobody has ever seen. The researchers only had DNA from dogs alive today, so the forks are worked out backwards from their descendants. In human evolution these unseen ancestors are called missing links: the ape that humans and gorillas both descend from, for example, has never been found.

Dogs have a missing link too. The grey wolf and the basenji share an ancestor: the Ice Age wolf population dogs came from, which has since died out. Watch the chart: if DNA from that population were found, it would join the tree right at the fork where dogs part from wolves, between the wolf and the basenji. Every breed shifts over to make room, but in this simple picture none changes places: a sample of that ancestor fills in its fork rather than rearranging the branches that grew from it. What it would add is a real, dated dog at that fork, instead of one inferred from its descendants. (In practice, ancient DNA can also reveal old mixing that redraws parts of a tree.)
